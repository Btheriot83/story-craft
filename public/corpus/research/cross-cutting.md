# Make a miniature murder movie.
A narrated factual story moves through people making consequential choices. The research decides what can be said; the structure decides when a listener needs to hear it.

## 01 · Learn: build the evidence packet
Begin with a named principal, their relationships, and the question the surviving evidence can actually answer. Find original records where available: judgments and appeals, inquest findings, trial transcripts, contemporaneous documents, and attributable interviews. Journalism and scholarship provide context and challenges; an official allegation is still an allegation.

Create a claim ledger before prose. Each entry needs an ID, exact claim, source URL or archive reference, page or time locator, speaker, date, evidentiary status, contradictions, and permitted wording. Useful statuses are documented fact, allegation, testimony, finding, later interpretation, disputed, and unknown. Keep a master timeline separate from the order of narration.

**Stop and research** when a central claim has no support, sources conflict in a way that changes the story, or a current case status cannot be established. State what is missing. Do not fill the gap with a plausible scene.

## 02 · Plan: choose the human spine
Complete this sentence: “Because [documented relationship or choice], [person] did [supported action], which changed [specific stakes].” Repeat through the story. If the links are only “and then,” you have a list of events, not yet a narrative.

Choose overlapping lenses as the evidence warrants: relationship and betrayal; death and investigation; trial and disputed truth. Affairs, jealousy, poison, wrongful-conviction questions and unresolved cases are subjects, not compulsory beats. Never manufacture romance, motive, guilt or a solution to fit a template.

Select four to six defining moments. For each, record: time, person, supported action, change in the listener’s understanding, supporting claim IDs, and uncertainty. Plan the last sentence now. It must return to a named person and a verified consequence, sourced revelation, or precisely framed unresolved question.

## 03 · Write: let action carry the explanation
Open with a specific supported action or contradiction. Establish who these people are to each other before adding procedural detail. A relationship earns attention through something it changes: access to a home, a decision to leave, a disputed alibi, a letter entered in evidence.

Use chronology by default. If the opening jumps forward, mark the return explicitly: “Three months earlier…” Then guide the listener back. Suspense may delay an answer; it must not hide an acquittal or decisive exculpatory fact in a way that falsely implies guilt.

Alternate action with brief reflection: what changed, why it matters, what remains unknown. Speak short, varied sentences aloud. Translate jargon on first use: an acquittal means the charge was not proved beyond reasonable doubt; it does not establish who committed the crime.

Do not invent dialogue, thoughts, attraction, injuries, physical details or motives. Attribute reconstruction and contested accounts. Avoid graphic detail unless it changes understanding. A victim must be a person, not a device for making an offender fascinating.

## 04 · Grade: evidence before elegance
Draft toward **570–610 narration words**. The target is **strictly longer than 3:00 through about 4:30**, with a **hard ceiling of 5:00**. These are editorial calibration targets, not a universal law or a timing guarantee. Time an actual read with pauses; a word-count estimate is only provisional. Revise the story rather than accelerating delivery.

Fifty scenes is a later production target. It is not a prose quota. This training ends with researched narration and evaluation, not shot lists, image prompts or media generation.

Use the [grading desk](/train) and the [universal checklist](/checklists/universal). An invented claim fails regardless of the craft score. An unresolved case may pass with an honest, specific ending.

# The letter that outlived the verdict
**Entirely fictional training case.** Every person, document, event and claim below is invented for this exercise. Nothing is evidence about a real person. The model demonstrates how a source packet constrains narration; never use its fictional records as factual sources.

## 1 · The practice packet
- **F1 · Fictional marriage register:** Ada Vale married Daniel Vale in 1928. She ran a dressmaking shop; he handled its accounts.
- **F2 · Fictional dated letter:** in March 1931 Ada wrote to her sister Ruth that she planned to leave Daniel and move the shop. She did not allege an affair.
- **F3 · Fictional inquest and laboratory report:** Ada died in April after becoming ill at home. Poison was reported in a retained sample. The report did not identify who administered it.
- **F4 · Fictional trial record:** prosecution alleged Daniel poisoned Ada to stop her leaving. A neighbor said Daniel carried a cup upstairs. Defense disputed identification and the sample’s chain of custody. Daniel denied the charge. Jury convicted.
- **F5 · Fictional appeal judgment:** one year later, conviction reversed because the judge had withheld a material warning about identification evidence. No finding of innocence or identity of a killer. Prosecution did not retry.
- **F6 · Fictional property record and Ruth’s attributable interview:** Ruth closed the shop after Ada’s death. The letter remained with Ruth. No later resolution appears in this practice packet.

## 2 · Plan before prose
**Question:** Why did a conviction survive only a year, and what did that reversal leave unresolved?

**Human spine:** Ada’s decision to leave → the allegation that her husband stopped her → a verdict shaped by disputed evidence → reversal without an answer for Ruth.

**Order:** open on the reversal; explicitly return to the marriage; proceed chronologically back to the reversal and its human consequence.

**Ending:** Ruth has Ada’s documented intention, but no established explanation for who caused her death. Do not claim the letter identifies a killer.

## 3 · Full narration draft
The court set Daniel Vale free a year after a jury convicted him of murdering his wife. It did not decide who had poisoned Ada. For her sister Ruth, the reversal left two records that could not answer each other: a judgment setting aside the conviction, and a letter in which Ada had said she was leaving.

Three years before Ada died, she and Daniel had married. She ran a dressmaking shop. He handled its accounts. Their marriage was also a working arrangement. Her plan to separate would involve more than ending that marriage: she intended to move the shop, too.

In March 1931, she wrote to Ruth about doing exactly that. The letter described a plan to leave Daniel and relocate the shop. It did not mention an affair. It did not predict a murder. What it preserved was a decision Ada had made about her own future, while that future was still hers to arrange.

The following month, Ada became ill at home and died. A laboratory reported poison in a retained sample. That finding gave the investigation a cause to examine. It did not identify the person who had administered the poison, or establish how it had reached her.

At Daniel’s trial, prosecutors connected the death to the separation. They argued that he had poisoned Ada to prevent her from leaving. The letter now had a second role: a record of her plans became part of an accusation about his motive. That accusation was the prosecution’s account. Daniel denied the charge.

The distinction matters. Ada could describe what she intended to do. Her letter could not establish what Daniel intended, or what he had done. To turn her decision into proof of murder required evidence beyond the decision itself.

A neighbor supplied another part of the case. The witness said Daniel had carried a cup upstairs. But a cup being carried was not the same thing as poison being administered. The defense disputed whether the neighbor had identified Daniel reliably. It also challenged the handling of the sample before the laboratory examined it.

Those objections mattered in different ways. One concerned who the witness had seen. The other concerned whether the sample could reliably be connected to the case. Neither objection, by itself, supplied another explanation for Ada’s death. Both challenged the prosecution’s attempt to connect Daniel to it.

The jury convicted him. At that point, the court had reached a verdict, but the questions about the evidence had not disappeared. Daniel appealed. A year later, the appeal court ruled that the trial judge had withheld a material warning about the identification evidence. The conviction could not stand.

That ruling was narrower than an answer to the murder. It did not find Daniel innocent. It did not identify somebody else as the killer. It established that the conviction had been reached through a trial whose treatment of a crucial witness was defective. Prosecutors did not try Daniel again.

Ruth had already closed Ada’s shop. The appeal could release Daniel, but it could not restore the business Ada had intended to carry into a different life. Nor did it tell Ruth whether the prosecution’s account of the death had been right.

The surviving record ends there. There is no later resolution in this account, and no sound basis for supplying one. Ada’s letter stayed with Ruth. It established that Ada had planned to leave her marriage. After a conviction, an appeal and Daniel’s release, it still could not tell her sister who had taken away the chance.

## 4 · What the structure is doing
- Opening: a consequential contradiction, not a lurid promise. F5 supports the reversal; F2 supplies the personal counterweight.
- “Three years before…” explicitly signals the flashback. F1–F3 then restore chronology.
- Marriage and shared work explain why leaving changes Ada’s life. No invented attraction, argument or secret lover.
- “Prosecutors… argued” and “the defense disputed” keep F4’s competing voices distinct.
- The appeal is explained in ordinary language without converting reversal into exoneration.
- The ending returns to Ruth and Ada’s unrealized plan. It does not solve the invented case by insinuation.
